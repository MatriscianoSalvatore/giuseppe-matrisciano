Who owns the knife? Segment by use, not by demographics.

I used to think marketing was mostly advertising. Then a final project in Consumer Analytics at the University of Georgia asked me to test a stereotype with real data.

The stereotype

When we think of knives, most people imagine men or older people owning them. I chose this topic because I wanted to test whether those assumptions are actually true, using real data instead of stereotypes. My question was simple: do demographics, such as gender or age, predict knife ownership?

The data

I worked with survey responses from 630 people. Each person gave demographic information, such as gender and age, and said whether they own at least one non-kitchen knife. The sample was balanced: 47% men and 53% women. That matters, because any difference I found would be more likely to reflect a real pattern than a skewed sample.

  • 630people in the dataset
  • 70%own at least one non-kitchen knife
  • 0.07the gap between men (3.38 knives) and women (3.31)

The first surprise was how common ownership is: 70% of the respondents, 443 people, own at least one non-kitchen knife.

Three ways of asking the same question

First I compared the averages. Men own about 3.38 knives and women about 3.31, a difference of 0.07. Then I ran a t-test: the p-value was 0.49, far above the 0.05 threshold, so the difference is not statistically significant.

I checked age with a correlation. It was very weak, with a p-value of 0.15. Younger and older adults in the sample own about the same number of knives.

Finally I put gender and age together in a multiple regression, to see whether one of them matters once the other is accounted for. Neither did: p = 0.22 for gender and p = 0.15 for age.

So the takeaway is simple. Demographics do not predict knife ownership. People seem to buy knives for practical reasons or hobbies, not because of who they are.

What I would do with it

  1. Avoid gender-targeted marketing. Marketing knives as products for men or for women would not match real consumer behavior, and it could limit the audience for no reason.
  2. Segment by use case, not demographics. Some people buy knives for hiking, others for work, others as a utility tool. Marketing should focus on the purpose, not the person.
  3. Show diverse users. Showing only men gives an inaccurate picture of the market. Social media campaigns should show different genders, ages and lifestyles.

What it changed for me

Before this class I thought marketing was mainly about advertising. The project showed me that data is just as important: companies need to understand consumers first, and then use creativity to develop effective strategies. The part I enjoyed most was translating statistical results into marketing recommendations.

Assumptions should never replace evidence when making business decisions.

The data explains the consumer; creativity turns it into strategy.
