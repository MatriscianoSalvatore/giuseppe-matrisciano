Consumer analytics · UGA n =630 Who owns the knife? Testing a stereotype with t-tests, correlation and multiple regression, then turning the result into strategy.

Research spotlight · Consumer Analytics, UGA

Who owns the knife?

I used to think marketing was mostly advertising. Then a final project asked me to test a stereotype with real data: do gender and age predict who owns non-kitchen knives?

Before the data — your guess

Who do you think owns more non-kitchen knives?

Men≈Women

Across 630 survey responses, men owned 3.38 non-kitchen knives on average and women 3.31. The t-test gave p = 0.49, nowhere near significant, and age showed only a weak correlation with ownership.

How I tested it

  1. 01Descriptive statistics
  2. 02t-tests
  3. 03Correlation analysis
  4. 04Multiple regression

630 participants
2 demographic variables
1 stereotype, retired

What the data said

  • 630people in the dataset: 47% men, 53% women
  • 70%own at least one non-kitchen knife. That's 443 people
  • 3.38 vs 3.31knives owned on average by men and by women. A gap of 0.07
  • 0significant predictors. Neither gender nor age explains who owns more
Four tests, one answer. A result counts as significant below p = 0.05.

From finding to strategy

A

Segment by use, not by demographics

People were buying knives for practical needs and hobbies. Traditional demographic segmentation was a poor predictor of behavior.

B

Talk to the people who actually use it

Target outdoor lovers, hobbyists and professionals who use knives in their daily activities, instead of assuming age or gender decides.

C

Open the campaign up

Create marketing that appeals to a broader range of consumers rather than relying on old stereotypes.

Assumptions should never replace evidence when making business decisions.

What the project taught me. The data explains the consumer; creativity turns it into strategy.

Team case analysis · MKT 695 Topics in Marketing · University of Miami · September 2026

Daily Crunch case

“Marketing a Start-Up During a Shutdown.” With three classmates I presented a digital-first, distribution-led growth strategy for a start-up that could no longer count on in-person sampling.

  1. The problem

    Daily Crunch launched in February 2020 with a plan built on in-person sampling: expos and in-store demos. One month later that channel was gone. The question for 2021 was how to get people to try a premium, unfamiliar snack without being in a store.

  2. What we decided

    Go digital-first and distribution-led. Scale the two channels already working, wholesale distribution and the email-driven website. Replace sampling with subscription boxes, influencer seeding and press coverage. Hold new flavors until distribution is solid.

  3. The result

    One recommendation with a roadmap and a risk plan, defended in front of the class in September 2026, in my first weeks of the M.S. in Marketing. It is a case analysis, so the result is the plan and the argument behind it.

The numbers behind the decision · from the case data

  • 50%of website sales already came from email
  • 10×Instagram followers in 2020, from 400 to 3,926, while website sales stayed flat
  • 9×wholesale revenue in November 2020 compared with October, right after distribution went live

My part · the 2021 roadmap, the risks and the close

  1. Q1

    Deepen the wholesale accounts and look for one or two new distributors. Move ad budget from paid social and Google toward email. Sign the first subscription-box and influencer partners.

  2. Q2

    Build landing pages tied to the Martha Stewart Living feature and other press. Track email growth and customer sales. Hold new products.

  3. Q3

    Bring on the new distributors if the first half goes well. Expand the influencer and subscription-box programs that are working. Check sales and cash before expanding.

  4. Q4

    Invest more in what worked and less in what didn't. Decide whether it's time for new flavors, and use the year's results to plan 2022.

The approach

We applied the Miami Method to define, prioritize and defend our recommendation: plot every option against business impact and effort, and keep the few that matter. The 80/20.

The risks I covered

Restrictions lasting longer, cheaper private-label competition, depending on a single distributor and a small founding team. Each came with an answer, from adding a second distributor to not launching new products yet.

Team campaign concept · MKT 640 Foundations of Marketing Management · University of Miami

RIMOWA: Your Best Travel Companion

A digital brand-building campaign for RIMOWA, developed with three classmates. The brief: change what people associate with a brand by letting customers carry the message, on a budget capped at $2,000.

  1. The problem

    RIMOWA means premium travel, luxury suitcases and German engineering. It also sells a pet carrier, but that product is not part of what people associate with the brand.

  2. What we decided

    Turn an existing RIMOWA store into a giant version of its own pet carrier, with a lightweight, removable façade. Inside, rescue puppies and a digital photo booth. Customers share their photos with #MyRIMOWACompanion and build a digital collage. No paid influencers.

  3. The result

    A pitch presented in class, with a 12-month plan in six phases and a way to prove whether it worked. It is a concept, so there are no live results to report.

The plan in numbers

  • $1,800estimated cost against a $2,000 cap, including a $300 contingency fund
  • 12months in six phases, from approvals to evaluation
  • 0paid influencers or paid social. Only customers' own content and RIMOWA's channels

My part · measurement

I presented how we would know if it worked. Ask the same open question before and after the experience, “What comes to mind when you think of RIMOWA?”, and compare how many people mention pets or pet carriers without being prompted.

My part · budget

I also presented the budget. We kept it at $1,800 by using an existing store instead of renting a venue, borrowing a tablet for the photo booth and relying on free tools such as Google Forms and Canva.

Focus

  • Social content
  • User-generated content
  • Digital brand storytelling

Why this brand

I'm drawn to technology and luxury: industries where innovation, branding and consumer-focused marketing all matter at once.
